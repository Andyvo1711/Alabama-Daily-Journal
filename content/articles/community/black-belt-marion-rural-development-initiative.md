In Marion, the county seat of Perry County, a coalition of local officials, business owners, and regional development advocates is working to reverse a decades-long trend of population decline that has affected much of Alabama's Black Belt region. The effort centers on a mix of infrastructure upgrades, small-business support, and workforce training aimed at giving residents more reasons to stay and outside investors more reasons to look at the area.

Like many Black Belt communities, Marion has faced persistent challenges including limited broadband access, aging water and sewer infrastructure, and a shrinking tax base as younger residents move to larger metro areas for work. Local leaders say addressing these fundamentals is essential before any broader economic development strategy can gain traction.

## Infrastructure as a Foundation

Much of the current effort has focused on securing funding for water system upgrades, some of which serve infrastructure that has been in place for generations. Aging pipes have led to frequent service interruptions in parts of the county, a problem that officials say discourages both residential growth and business investment.

Broadband expansion has also emerged as a priority, with several unserved pockets of the county still lacking reliable internet access. Local officials have been working with state and federal rural broadband programs to secure funding for line extensions, arguing that without connectivity, remote work opportunities and modern farming technology remain out of reach for many residents.

A county development official said the basic infrastructure conversation may not be as exciting as recruiting a new employer, but without reliable water and internet service, attracting any kind of sustained investment becomes nearly impossible.

### Supporting Small Businesses and Agriculture

Beyond infrastructure, the initiative includes support for small businesses along Marion's historic downtown square, several of which have struggled to stay open amid declining foot traffic. A small grant program has helped a handful of business owners with facade improvements and basic equipment upgrades, while a local chamber affiliate has organized periodic downtown events aimed at drawing shoppers from across the county.

Agricultural support has also featured prominently, given the county's continued reliance on farming as an economic base. Extension programs have offered workshops on diversifying crops and exploring value-added products, such as processed goods that can be sold directly to consumers rather than solely through commodity markets, potentially offering farmers better margins.

## Building on Local Institutions

Marion is also home to several historic institutions, including church congregations and educational institutions with deep community ties, which local leaders see as anchors for the broader development effort. Partnerships between these institutions and development organizers have included workforce training sessions and community meetings aimed at gathering resident input on priorities.

Organizers acknowledge that reversing long-term population decline is a slow process without guaranteed outcomes, and they are careful not to overpromise rapid results. Still, they point to incremental progress, including new broadband connections reaching previously unserved households and a handful of small business openings downtown, as signs that the strategy is beginning to gain traction.

Looking ahead, county officials say they plan to continue pursuing state and federal rural development grants while working to strengthen coordination between the county, the city of Marion, and community organizations. They describe the work as a long-term commitment rather than a quick fix, one that will require sustained attention across multiple budget cycles to show meaningful results for a community that has weathered economic headwinds for generations.
