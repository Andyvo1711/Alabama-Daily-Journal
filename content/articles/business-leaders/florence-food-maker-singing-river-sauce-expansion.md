Odessa Marlow still remembers cooking her first batches of hot sauce in a rented slot at a shared commercial kitchen in Florence, squeezing production time between other small food businesses using the same equipment. This spring, Singing River Sauce Co. moved into its own dedicated production facility, a milestone Marlow says reflects years of slowly building relationships with regional grocers one store at a time.

"I used to have to schedule my whole life around a four-hour kitchen slot twice a week," Marlow said. "Having our own space means we can actually plan production around what our customers need instead of around somebody else's calendar."

## From Farmers Markets to Grocery Shelves

Marlow started the company selling bottled hot sauces and pepper jellies at farmers markets across the Shoals area, developing recipes based on family cooking traditions passed down from her grandmother. As demand grew beyond what farmers market sales alone could generate, she began approaching small independent grocers about carrying her products on their shelves.

"Getting into that first store felt harder than starting the whole business," Marlow said. "Once one store said yes, it got a little easier to convince the next one, but that first conversation took months."

Over several years, Singing River Sauce Co.'s products have gradually expanded into grocery stores across north Alabama and into parts of neighboring Tennessee, a footprint Marlow said grew almost entirely through word of mouth and relationships built directly with store owners rather than through a formal sales team.

## The Challenges of Scaling a Food Business

Moving from farmers market volumes to grocery store distribution required Marlow to navigate food safety certifications, co-packing considerations, and consistent recipe standardization, challenges she said caught her off guard early on. A batch that tasted slightly different from one week to the next might be forgivable at a farmers market stand, she said, but grocery customers expect a consistent product every time.

"People don't want their favorite hot sauce to taste different depending on which batch they happened to buy," Marlow said. "We had to get much more precise about our processes than we ever needed to be selling directly at a market table."

The new production facility includes upgraded bottling equipment and additional storage space, allowing Singing River Sauce Co. to fulfill larger grocery orders without running into the scheduling constraints of the shared kitchen space. Marlow said the move also allows the company to maintain tighter quality control since it no longer has to break down and reset equipment between rented kitchen sessions.

## Staying Connected to Local Roots

Despite the growth, Marlow said she has intentionally kept the company's recipes and branding rooted in the Shoals-area food traditions that inspired the business in the first place, resisting suggestions from a few retail buyers to simplify or "mainstream" some of her spicier, more distinctive flavors.

"Some people wanted us to make a milder, more generic sauce that would sell to a broader audience," Marlow said. "But that's not what got us into these stores in the first place. People wanted something that tasted like it actually came from somewhere."

Marlow said the company plans to add one or two new product lines over the coming year, building on the increased production capacity the new facility provides, though she remains cautious about growing distribution too quickly beyond the current regional footprint.

"I'd rather be really good in the stores we're already in than spread too thin trying to be everywhere at once," she said.
