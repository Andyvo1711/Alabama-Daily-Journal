For years, a cluster of lots along one of Birmingham's transitional commercial corridors sat empty, fenced off and overgrown between older storefronts still hanging on. Today, framing rises on two of those parcels, the first phase of a mixed-use redevelopment led by Ironvine Construction Group, a Birmingham-based builder that has spent the last several years focused specifically on infill projects like this one.

"Nobody wants to be the first developer on a block that's been empty for fifteen years," said Talia Ashworth, who founded Ironvine after working for larger regional developers earlier in her career. "But somebody has to be, or the block stays empty for another fifteen."

## Small-Scale Development With Big Ambitions

Ashworth's approach differs from the large single-use developments that have defined much of Birmingham's growth over the past decade. Instead, Ironvine has focused on smaller mixed-use blocks that combine ground-floor retail or restaurant space with residential units above, a format she said is better suited to filling in gaps between existing neighborhoods rather than building entirely new districts from scratch.

"We're not trying to build the next big entertainment district," Ashworth said. "We're trying to stitch existing neighborhoods back together with buildings that feel like they could have always been there."

The current project involves three parcels that had been vacant for over a decade, complicated by unclear ownership history and deferred infrastructure repairs that made them unattractive to larger developers focused on bigger, cleaner sites. Ashworth said Ironvine spent nearly two years just resolving title issues and coordinating with the city before construction could begin.

## Navigating the Realities of Infill Construction

Building on scattered urban lots comes with challenges that greenfield development doesn't, according to Ashworth, including aging utility infrastructure, tighter site access for construction equipment, and closer proximity to existing residents who are directly affected by construction noise and traffic.

"You can't just show up with heavy equipment and not think about the fact that people live and work right next door," she said. "We've had to get good at communicating with neighbors before we ever break ground, not after complaints start coming in."

Ashworth said Ironvine has held informal meetings with nearby business owners and residents ahead of each phase of construction, a practice she credits with reducing friction compared to the company's earlier projects, where community pushback sometimes stalled permitting.

## Betting on Birmingham's Underused Corridors

Ashworth said she sees dozens of similar opportunities scattered across Birmingham's older commercial corridors, parcels that are too small individually to interest large developers but that, in clusters, could support meaningful new housing and retail space without requiring large-scale demolition or displacement.

"There's a version of growth that means bulldozing whole blocks, and there's a version that means filling in what's already missing," she said. "I think the second version is a lot healthier for a city long-term."

The current project is expected to bring a mix of apartments and small commercial spaces to the corridor, with Ashworth saying she has already fielded interest from a handful of local business owners looking to lease the ground-floor retail space once construction wraps.

For Ashworth, the appeal of this kind of work goes beyond the financial return. She grew up a few miles from the current project site, she said, and remembers when the now-vacant lots were still active businesses.

"I'm not just building buildings," Ashworth said. "I'm trying to help a place I grew up in feel whole again."
