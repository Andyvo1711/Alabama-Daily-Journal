When Genevieve Sorrell first walked through the century-old house near downtown Fairhope that would become the Bay Laurel Inn, half the floorboards were soft with water damage and the wraparound porch sagged noticeably on one side. Three years and a painstaking restoration later, the property has become a small boutique inn drawing guests from across the Gulf Coast, many of them repeat visitors.

"Everyone told me I was buying a teardown," Sorrell said. "I could see past the water damage to what the house wanted to be again."

## A Restoration Done in Stages

Sorrell, who previously worked in interior design before shifting into hospitality, said she approached the renovation deliberately slowly, restoring the property room by room rather than gutting it all at once. That pace, she said, allowed her to preserve original architectural details like the home's heart pine flooring and hand-carved staircase banister, elements that a faster renovation might have sacrificed for convenience.

"You can always fix a house fast if you don't care what you lose in the process," Sorrell said. "I cared a lot about what we lost, so we went slow."

The inn now has eight guest rooms, each individually decorated and named after local landmarks and native coastal plants. Sorrell said she deliberately avoided a uniform, corporate hotel aesthetic, wanting each room to feel distinct enough that returning guests would request specific ones by name.

## Building a Business Around Slow Travel

Sorrell has positioned the Bay Laurel Inn around what she calls "slow travel," marketing the property to guests looking for an unhurried weekend rather than a quick overnight stay near the highway. The inn's small size, she said, allows staff to build genuine familiarity with returning guests, something larger hotels along the coast struggle to replicate.

"We know when someone's anniversary is. We know if they like their coffee on the porch instead of the dining room," Sorrell said. "That's not something you can do at scale, and I don't want to do it at scale."

The inn has also built partnerships with several small businesses in downtown Fairhope, including a local bakery that supplies breakfast pastries and a bookstore that curates a small lending library for guest rooms. Sorrell said those relationships reflect her broader philosophy that the inn's success is tied to the health of the surrounding downtown business community.

## Weathering the Challenges of a Small Hospitality Business

Running a small independent inn has not been without its difficulties, Sorrell acknowledged, particularly around staffing during peak season and managing the unpredictable costs of maintaining a century-old structure. A leaking roof section discovered during the property's second summer required an unplanned repair that Sorrell said tested the business's finances more than she expected.

"Old houses keep surprising you, even after you think you've found everything," she said. "You have to keep some cushion in the budget for that, always."

Despite the challenges, Sorrell said occupancy has grown steadily since the inn opened, driven largely by word of mouth and return visitors rather than large marketing campaigns. She has resisted pressure from a few interested parties to expand into a second property, at least for now, preferring to focus on maintaining the quality of the original inn.

"I didn't restore this house to turn around and build five more like it," Sorrell said. "I want to get this one exactly right first."
