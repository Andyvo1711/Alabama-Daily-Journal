The University of Alabama at Birmingham is expanding its graduate medical education programs, adding residency positions across several specialties as state health officials continue to warn about physician shortages in both rural and urban parts of Alabama. The expansion touches internal medicine, family medicine, and several surgical subspecialties, according to the university's health system.

Medical education leaders have long pointed to residency placement as one of the strongest predictors of where a young physician ultimately settles down to practice. Doctors who complete residency training in Alabama are considerably more likely to remain in the state afterward compared with those who leave for training elsewhere, which has made expanding in-state residency capacity a priority for UAB's health system administrators.

## Why Residency Slots Matter So Much

Unlike medical school admissions, which are shaped largely by the number of applicants a school chooses to accept, residency training slots are constrained by federal funding formulas and the availability of teaching hospital capacity. That has made it difficult for academic medical centers, including UAB, to expand residency programs quickly even when there is clear demand from both students and rural hospitals desperate for physicians.

UAB's health system has said the newly added positions were made possible partly through partnerships with regional hospitals that agreed to serve as additional training sites, effectively spreading residents across a wider network of clinical settings rather than concentrating all training within the main Birmingham campus.

### Addressing the Rural Physician Gap

Alabama's rural counties have faced physician shortages for years, with some communities losing their only local doctor to retirement without a clear successor. Public health officials have repeatedly cited the shortage as a contributing factor to worse health outcomes in rural areas, particularly for chronic conditions that benefit from consistent, nearby primary care.

As part of the residency expansion, UAB has emphasized rotations that place residents in rural and underserved clinics for extended stretches of their training, a model medical educators believe increases the odds that trainees will consider practicing in similar communities after they finish. The university's health system noted in a statement that exposure to rural practice during training has historically correlated with a greater willingness among new physicians to accept rural positions.

## A Broader Statewide Strategy

The residency expansion is occurring alongside other statewide efforts to address the physician pipeline, including loan repayment programs for doctors who commit to practicing in underserved counties and scholarship incentives at Alabama's medical schools tied to rural service commitments. UAB officials have described their residency expansion as complementary to those programs rather than a replacement for them.

Hospital administrators in Birmingham say the expanded residency program also benefits the city's existing health system by increasing clinical capacity, since resident physicians provide a substantial share of patient care within teaching hospitals under the supervision of attending physicians.

The timeline for fully implementing the new residency positions will unfold over several years, since medical residencies typically run three to seven years depending on specialty. UAB has said it expects the full effects of the expansion, in terms of new physicians entering the Alabama workforce, to become apparent gradually rather than immediately. University officials have said they view the investment as a long-term commitment to the state's health care infrastructure rather than a short-term fix for an immediate staffing gap.
