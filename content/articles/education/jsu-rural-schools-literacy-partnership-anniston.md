Jacksonville State University has expanded a literacy support partnership with several rural school districts near Anniston, sending education students and faculty into elementary classrooms to assist with reading instruction. The effort comes amid continued statewide attention to early literacy following changes to Alabama's reading proficiency requirements in recent years, which have put additional pressure on schools to identify and support struggling readers earlier.

University officials say the partnership grew out of conversations with district leaders who described a shortage of specialized reading intervention staff, particularly in smaller rural systems that lack the budget to hire dedicated literacy coaches for every elementary school. JSU's College of Education has positioned its students and faculty as a supplemental resource, working alongside existing classroom teachers rather than replacing them.

## A Response to Statewide Literacy Pressure

Alabama's push to improve early reading outcomes has placed particular strain on smaller and rural districts, which often have fewer resources to implement intensive reading intervention programs compared with larger suburban systems. Superintendents in several counties near Anniston have said recruiting and retaining reading specialists has been difficult, especially in schools serving high numbers of students who need additional support.

By embedding JSU students trained in literacy instruction into these classrooms, the university aims to provide extra hands for small-group and one-on-one reading support, while also giving its own education majors direct experience with evidence-based literacy interventions. Faculty overseeing the program say the partnership benefits both the participating schools and the university's own teacher preparation goals simultaneously.

### Training Future Teachers in Literacy Instruction

JSU faculty have emphasized that the literacy partnership is not simply about providing extra staffing to rural schools, but also about ensuring that the university's own graduates leave with strong, practical skills in reading instruction. Coursework tied to the partnership includes training in structured literacy approaches, an area of increasing emphasis nationally as more schools shift toward phonics-based instructional methods.

Education faculty say the hands-on component gives students far more exposure to real reading intervention work than classroom coursework alone could provide, particularly when it comes to assessing why an individual student might be struggling and selecting an appropriate intervention strategy.

## Looking Ahead for Rural Districts

District administrators involved in the partnership have said the additional support has been especially valuable in schools where a single reading specialist might otherwise be responsible for supporting struggling readers across multiple grade levels. While the JSU students are not replacing certified literacy specialists, their presence allows existing staff to focus more intensively on students with the most significant needs.

University officials say they hope to expand the partnership to additional districts in the surrounding region over time, contingent on funding and the availability of faculty to supervise the placements. The program's structure requires significant coordination between university scheduling and individual school calendars, a logistical challenge the university says it has worked through carefully in the initial rollout.

For rural districts near Anniston already stretched thin on specialized staffing, the partnership offers a low-cost supplement to existing literacy efforts. University leaders have framed the initiative as consistent with Jacksonville State's broader identity as a regional institution deeply tied to the school systems that surround its campus, rather than a program aimed primarily at generating research or academic publications detached from direct community impact.
