Alabama A&M University, based just outside Huntsville in Normal, has expanded partnerships with several rural school districts in north Alabama aimed at getting education majors into real classrooms earlier in their academic careers. The initiative comes as districts across the state, particularly in rural counties, continue to report difficulty filling teaching positions in subjects like math, science, and special education.

University officials in the College of Education say the partnerships allow students to begin classroom observation and limited teaching responsibilities well before their formal student-teaching semester, giving them a longer runway to decide whether a teaching career is the right fit and to build relationships with the districts where they may eventually seek employment.

## Addressing a Statewide Shortage

Alabama's rural school districts have struggled for years to compete with better-resourced suburban and urban systems when it comes to recruiting and retaining teachers, particularly in hard-to-staff subject areas. Superintendents in several north Alabama counties have said openings in math and science routinely go unfilled for stretches of the school year, forcing schools to rely on long-term substitutes or teachers working outside their certification area.

By embedding Alabama A&M students in rural classrooms earlier and more consistently, university officials hope to build familiarity and connection between the future teachers and the communities where shortages are most acute. Education faculty at the university have noted that student teachers who complete placements in rural districts are more likely to consider accepting a position there after graduation than those who only observe urban or suburban schools.

### Structuring the Placements

The expanded partnership includes a tiered system of classroom involvement, starting with observation-only visits for students earlier in their coursework and gradually increasing responsibility as students progress toward their degree. District administrators have worked with university faculty to identify mentor teachers willing to take on the additional responsibility of supervising undergraduates throughout the year rather than only during a single student-teaching semester.

Transportation has been one of the more practical challenges in expanding the program, given the distances between the Huntsville-area campus and some of the rural districts involved. The university has said it is working with participating districts to coordinate scheduling that minimizes the burden of travel on students who may also be juggling other coursework and, in some cases, part-time jobs.

## A Pipeline With Broader Implications

Alabama A&M's approach reflects a growing recognition among teacher preparation programs statewide that traditional student-teaching models, which place aspiring educators in classrooms for only a single semester near the end of their degree, may not be sufficient to prepare or retain teachers for the state's most challenging placements. Other universities in Alabama have experimented with similar early-immersion models, though the scale and specific structure vary by institution.

University leaders describe the rural partnership as consistent with Alabama A&M's historical mission of serving communities that have often been underserved by other institutions, extending that mission into the realm of teacher preparation. Education faculty have said they hope to track outcomes over the coming years, including how many participating students ultimately accept teaching positions in the rural districts where they trained.

For the participating school districts, the partnership offers a low-cost way to build a homegrown talent pipeline at a time when recruiting experienced teachers from outside the region has become increasingly difficult. District officials have said they view the relationship with Alabama A&M as a long-term investment rather than a quick fix for current staffing gaps.
