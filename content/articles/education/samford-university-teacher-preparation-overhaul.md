Samford University in Homewood has redesigned its teacher preparation program, restructuring coursework to give education majors more time in actual classrooms earlier in their degree path. The changes come as Alabama school districts, including several in the Birmingham metro area, continue to report difficulty filling teaching vacancies, particularly in special education and secondary science.

Faculty in Samford's education department say the redesign was driven partly by feedback from school districts that hire the university's graduates, who indicated that new teachers often benefit from more sustained classroom exposure than a single student-teaching semester can provide. The new curriculum spreads classroom placements across multiple semesters rather than concentrating them entirely near the end of a student's program.

## Rethinking When Students Enter the Classroom

Traditionally, teacher preparation programs across Alabama have followed a similar structure: coursework and theory dominate the early college years, with intensive classroom placement reserved for a capstone student-teaching semester just before graduation. Samford's revised approach instead introduces structured classroom observation and limited teaching responsibilities as early as a student's sophomore year.

Education faculty say the earlier exposure serves two purposes. First, it gives students a more realistic sense of classroom management and daily teaching demands before they have fully committed to the profession, potentially reducing the number of students who complete a degree only to decide teaching is not the right fit. Second, it allows students to build teaching skills incrementally, rather than facing the steep learning curve that can come with a single, high-stakes student-teaching semester.

### Working With Birmingham-Area Districts

The redesigned program relies heavily on partnerships with school districts in and around Birmingham, including systems that have historically struggled with vacancy rates in certain subject areas. Samford has coordinated with district administrators to identify mentor teachers willing to supervise students across a longer stretch of their preparation rather than only during the traditional final placement.

University officials describe the partnerships as a two-way relationship: districts get additional support in classrooms from supervised undergraduates, while Samford gains placement sites that allow its students to build long-term relationships with schools where they may eventually seek employment after graduation and licensure.

## Addressing Concerns About New Teacher Retention

Alabama education officials have pointed to new teacher attrition, particularly within the first few years of a teaching career, as a significant contributor to ongoing staffing shortages. Faculty involved in designing Samford's revised curriculum say part of the motivation was addressing this attrition by better preparing students for the realities of classroom teaching before they accept a full-time position.

The redesigned program also incorporates additional coursework on classroom management and student behavioral support, areas that new teachers frequently cite as underprepared for despite strong content knowledge in their subject area. Faculty say the goal is producing graduates who feel more confident handling the day-to-day demands of a classroom, not just the academic material they are responsible for teaching.

Samford has said it will monitor outcomes from the redesigned program over the coming years, including retention rates among graduates during their first several years of teaching. University leadership has framed the curriculum overhaul as part of a broader institutional commitment to strengthening Alabama's teacher pipeline at a time when many districts across the state, urban and rural alike, continue to grapple with persistent staffing challenges.
