In Selma, a community health clinic has expanded its hours and provider staffing in an effort to fill gaps left by years of shrinking healthcare infrastructure across Dallas County, a rural stretch of Alabama's Black Belt region that has long ranked among the state's most underserved areas for medical care.

Rural healthcare access has been a persistent challenge across this part of Alabama, where hospital closures and provider shortages have left some residents driving considerable distances for care that would be a short trip away in more urban parts of the state. Community clinic administrators say the expanded services are meant to serve as a more accessible front line for primary and preventive care, reducing the need for some residents to travel to Montgomery or Birmingham for routine medical needs.

## A Region Shaped by Hospital Closures

Dallas County has seen its healthcare landscape shift substantially over the past decade, with the local hospital reducing certain services and, at various points, facing financial pressures that raised concerns among residents about the long-term availability of inpatient care in Selma. Against that backdrop, community clinics have taken on a larger role in delivering care that might otherwise fall through the cracks, including chronic disease management, basic diagnostic testing, and referrals for specialty care.

Clinic staff describe a patient population that includes a significant share of individuals without private insurance, many of whom have historically delayed seeking care until a condition became severe enough to require an emergency room visit. By expanding hours and adding providers, the clinic hopes to make routine and preventive visits more accessible, potentially catching health problems earlier and reducing the burden on the local emergency department.

## Addressing Transportation and Workforce Challenges

Transportation remains one of the most significant barriers to care in rural Alabama counties like Dallas, where public transit options are limited and many residents, particularly older adults, may not have reliable access to a vehicle. Clinic administrators say they have explored partnerships with local organizations to help arrange transportation for patients who would otherwise struggle to reach appointments.

Recruiting healthcare providers willing to practice in rural areas has also proven difficult, a challenge shared by community clinics and rural hospitals throughout the state. The clinic in Selma has leaned on a mix of strategies to address staffing, including recruiting providers through loan repayment programs that incentivize practice in medically underserved areas, as well as incorporating telehealth to supplement in-person visits for certain specialty consultations that would otherwise require a trip to a larger city.

## A Broader Pattern Across the Black Belt

Selma's experience mirrors that of other communities across Alabama's Black Belt, a region stretching across the central and western parts of the state that has historically faced some of the starkest health disparities in Alabama, including higher rates of chronic disease and lower life expectancy compared with state averages. Public health researchers have pointed to a combination of factors behind these disparities, including limited access to care, economic conditions, and a shortage of specialists willing to practice in rural settings.

Clinic leaders say they view their expanded services as one piece of a much larger effort needed to address rural health disparities in the region, one that will likely require continued investment from state and federal programs aimed at supporting rural healthcare infrastructure. For now, the clinic says it is focused on the immediate goal of making sure Dallas County residents have a reliable, accessible place to seek care close to home, rather than delaying treatment until a routine health issue becomes a medical emergency.
