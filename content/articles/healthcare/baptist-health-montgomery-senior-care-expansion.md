As Montgomery's population continues to age, Baptist Health has moved to expand the services it offers older adults, adding geriatric-focused programming intended to keep seniors healthier at home and reduce avoidable hospital admissions. The expansion touches several parts of the health system, from primary care to care coordination for patients managing multiple chronic conditions at once.

Hospital administrators describe the effort as a response to demographic trends that have been building for years across central Alabama, where a growing share of residents are now over the age of 65. That shift has pushed local health systems to rethink how they structure care for a population that often juggles several conditions simultaneously, including diabetes, heart disease, and mobility limitations.

## Coordinated Care for Complex Patients

A central piece of the expansion involves care coordination teams that work with older patients to manage transitions between hospital stays, specialist visits, and home care. Health system officials say fragmented care, where a patient sees multiple providers who don't communicate closely with one another, is a common source of complications and repeat hospitalizations among seniors.

Under the expanded model, designated care coordinators follow patients across settings, helping ensure medication lists stay consistent and that follow-up appointments actually happen after a hospital discharge. Baptist Health has said early internal data suggests this kind of coordination can meaningfully reduce the odds of a senior patient being readmitted within a month of leaving the hospital, though officials caution that outcomes vary widely depending on a patient's underlying health status.

## Addressing Falls, Frailty, and Isolation

Beyond medical management, the expanded senior services include screening for fall risk and frailty, two factors that geriatric specialists consider strong predictors of hospitalization among older adults. Patients identified as higher risk are referred to physical therapy or home safety evaluations, with the goal of catching hazards before they result in an injury.

The health system has also acknowledged the role that social isolation plays in senior health outcomes, noting that patients who live alone or lack regular family support often face worse outcomes even when their medical care is otherwise adequate. Baptist Health said it is working with community organizations across Montgomery to help connect isolated seniors with local resources, including transportation assistance and meal programs, as a complement to clinical care.

## A Regional Pattern of Investment in Aging Care

Montgomery's experience mirrors a broader pattern across Alabama, where health systems in cities including Mobile, Huntsville, and Tuscaloosa have similarly expanded geriatric and senior-focused programming in recent years. Officials at Baptist Health say the investment reflects both demographic necessity and a recognition that senior care, when done well, can reduce costly emergency department visits and hospital stays over time.

The health system has indicated it plans to continue building out its geriatric services over the next several years, including potential expansion of home-based primary care for homebound seniors who struggle to travel to a clinic. Officials say they are also evaluating partnerships with local senior living communities to bring more coordinated medical care directly to residents.

For families in Montgomery navigating care for aging parents, the expanded offerings represent an attempt to make that process less fragmented. Baptist Health leaders describe the effort as an ongoing one, with adjustments expected as the health system gathers more data on which interventions most effectively keep older patients healthy and out of the hospital.
