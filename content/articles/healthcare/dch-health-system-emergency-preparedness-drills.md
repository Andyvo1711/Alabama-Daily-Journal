DCH Health System in Tuscaloosa has run a series of emergency preparedness drills in recent weeks, simulating scenarios ranging from severe weather events to mass casualty incidents as part of an ongoing effort to keep the hospital system's disaster response plans current.

Tuscaloosa's history with severe weather, including a devastating tornado that struck the city more than a decade ago, has left a lasting mark on how local institutions approach emergency planning. For DCH Health System, which operates as the primary hospital network serving the city and surrounding West Alabama communities, preparedness drills are treated as a routine but essential part of hospital operations rather than a box-checking exercise.

## Testing Coordination Across Departments

The recent drills involved coordination across multiple hospital departments, including the emergency department, surgical services, facilities management, and administrative leadership. Officials say the exercises are designed to test not just clinical response but also logistical questions, such as how quickly the hospital could receive a surge of patients, where additional supplies would come from, and how communication would be maintained if primary systems were disrupted during a storm.

Hospital staff practiced scenarios involving simultaneous patient surges, testing how quickly triage teams could be assembled and how efficiently patients could be sorted by severity of injury. Officials say these kinds of drills are particularly important for a health system serving an area prone to severe spring weather, since real mass casualty events tend to unfold with little warning.

## Learning From Past Storms

DCH Health System officials say the drills draw on lessons learned from previous severe weather events in the region, including past tornado outbreaks that tested hospital capacity in Tuscaloosa and surrounding counties. Emergency planners say one recurring lesson from past events is the importance of backup communication systems, since cell networks and even landlines can become overwhelmed or damaged during a major storm.

The health system has also emphasized supply chain resilience as part of its preparedness planning, reviewing how quickly the hospital could restock critical medical supplies if regular delivery routes were disrupted by damaged roads or regional shortages following a widespread disaster. Officials say maintaining a sufficient on-site reserve of essential supplies, from blood products to basic wound care materials, is a key part of the hospital's disaster planning.

## Coordination With Regional and State Partners

DCH Health System has also worked to coordinate its emergency planning with regional and state emergency management officials, part of a broader network of hospitals across Alabama that participate in mutual aid arrangements during major disasters. Under such arrangements, hospitals in unaffected areas can accept transferred patients if a storm overwhelms capacity at a hospital in the direct path of severe weather.

Officials say the drills also incorporate scenario planning for events beyond severe weather, including preparedness for infectious disease outbreaks and other public health emergencies that could strain hospital resources in different ways than a storm-related mass casualty event.

Hospital leaders describe emergency preparedness as an ongoing process rather than a one-time achievement, noting that staff turnover, changes in hospital infrastructure, and evolving best practices all require regular retesting of disaster plans. DCH Health System said it plans to continue conducting regular drills throughout the year, with additional exercises planned ahead of the spring severe weather season that has historically posed the greatest risk to the Tuscaloosa area.
