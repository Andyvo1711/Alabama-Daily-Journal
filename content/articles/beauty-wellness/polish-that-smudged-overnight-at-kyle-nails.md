Two things quietly decide whether a client trusts a salon: whether the work holds up after they leave, and how the salon responds when it doesn't. This report on Kyle Nails touches both, and includes an unusual follow-up that turned an ordinary complaint into a lasting one.

## Polish That Smudged Hours After the Visit

The client came in for a simple pedicure with classic polish. The service itself was fine, if unremarkable. The problem showed up much later: she went home, went to bed, and woke up to find all of the polish smudged from the blankets. Regular polish that's still soft enough to smudge after many hours points to a drying or setting problem, not to anything the client did overnight. She also noted a telling detail: while she was in the salon, three different women came in asking to have their polish fixed. That pattern suggests the issue wasn't a one-off, but something in the product or process worth the salon's attention.

## Blamed for It, and a Refund Refused

Rather than taking ownership, the salon told the client the smudging was her fault and refused a refund. They did offer to fix the polish, which is a reasonable gesture, but by that point she'd lost confidence, understandably wondering why she should trust polish to hold up when it had smudged hours after leaving. Blaming the customer for what looks like a drying issue, especially against the backdrop of other clients needing fixes that same day, turns a fixable service problem into a trust problem.

## A Phone Call to Say the Review Was "Wrong"

The most striking part came after. Once the client submitted her review and survey, an employee called her to say she was wrong and to claim they would post security footage of how many people had asked for a fix. Contacting a customer to argue that their honest review is "wrong," and raising the idea of posting footage, is a serious misstep in reputation management. It reads as combative rather than caring, and it tends to do far more damage than the original complaint ever would.

## Final Thoughts

The lessons here are clear and very fixable. First, make sure polish is fully dried and set before a client leaves, using adequate dry time, a quick-dry top coat or drops, and a friendly heads-up about handling nails gently for the first stretch. Second, own problems rather than assigning blame. When work fails, the professional response is empathy and a remedy, a fix or a refund, not "that was your fault." Third, treat a pattern as data: several clients asking for fixes in one day is a signal to review the product and process, not a coincidence. And finally, never contact a reviewer to argue. Reviews are best answered calmly and publicly, with a genuine apology and an invitation to make it right. Handled that way, this pedicure would have been a minor hiccup instead of a story a customer now shares as a warning.
