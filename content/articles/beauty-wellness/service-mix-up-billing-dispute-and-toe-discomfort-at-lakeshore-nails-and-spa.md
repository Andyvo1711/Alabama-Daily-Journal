A client at Lakeshore Nails and Spa recently described a long visit that ended in frustration. She had planned her day carefully, booking a lash appointment for the afternoon and arriving at 11:30 after being told her nails would be finished by 1:00. She also said her toenails "were in great shape" before the visit. Her account touches on confirming what is being booked, how a bill is built, and how a disagreement is handled.

## Confirming What Is Being Booked

The client says she came in for a manicure fill-in and the replacement of one nail. According to her, a pedicure was then part of the visit, and she says she did not ask for one. She describes the polish change and the nail replacement as the only work done on her hands, with no cutting down or shaping of her nails. When a visit involves more than one service, a quick spoken summary at the start prevents misunderstanding: "Today we are doing a fill-in, one replacement nail, and a polish change. Is that right?" Writing the services on a ticket that the client can see adds a second layer of clarity. If a client is moved from one service to another during the visit, asking before beginning is always the safest way to go.

## How the Bill Was Built

By her account, the salon asked for $145, she declined to pay for the pedicure, and she paid $100 in the end. She felt this was still too much for the work she received. She also said she was at the salon for over two hours, which caused her to miss a lash appointment she had prepaid at $75. A bill dispute at the front desk is stressful for everyone, and the best protection is an estimate before the work begins and an itemized receipt at the end. When a client questions a charge, a calm walk through each line, with a willingness to adjust where it makes sense, keeps the moment from escalating. Estimating how long a visit will take, and updating the client if it runs long, also helps her plan the rest of her day.

## Toe Discomfort and the Tone of the Conversation

The client reports that one toe is swollen where it was cut and filed, which she believes was done carelessly. She also described the owner as rude and egotistical. The review cannot show what was said or what caused the swelling, but it does show how important tone is when a client raises a concern. If a client reports discomfort after cutting or filing, the best response is to pause, apologize, look at the area together, and offer simple first-aid care. A manager or owner who listens first, stays courteous, and offers a next step, such as a follow-up call or a correction, can often cool a tense exchange. Clients who need to spend more time and money fixing a problem later are likely to remember how they were treated at the desk.

## Final Thoughts

Three ideas stand out. First, confirm every service out loud and on a ticket before starting, and ask before adding anything, so the client is never surprised by the bill. Second, give honest time estimates and itemized receipts, so a long visit and a final total both make sense. Third, respond to concerns about discomfort or price with calm, respectful listening, and offer a clear path to make things right. A salon that handles a disagreement well can keep a client who might otherwise leave and tell others to go elsewhere.
